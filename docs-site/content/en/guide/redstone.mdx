Every face of the computer is a side a program can read and drive. The `redstone` library (or `rs`, which is the same table) does it.

## Naming the sides

Sides are named from the computer's front, the face with the screen: `front`, `back`, `left`, `right`, `top`, `bottom`. Left and right are as you see them standing in front of the computer. Turn the computer with a wrench and the names turn with it, so a program keeps working after you rotate it.

The back takes the shaft, so in practice redstone goes through the other five sides.

## Reading

`rs.getInput(side)` is `true` when any signal comes in on that side. `rs.getAnalogInput(side)` gives its strength, 0 to 15, which is what you want for comparators and Create's analog levers.

```lua
-- a lever on the left, a comparator reading a chest on the right
if rs.getInput("left") then
  print("lever is on")
end
print("chest fill level: " .. rs.getAnalogInput("right"))
```

The **Redstone** panel in the editor shows the signal coming in and going out on every side, live.

## Sending

`rs.setOutput(side, true)` sends a full-strength signal; `false` turns it off. `rs.setAnalogOutput(side, n)` sends any strength from 0 to 15.

Outputs are strong signals. Besides the block next to that side, they reach through it: a lamp behind a stone block on the computer's left still lights.

An output stays on until the program changes it, or until you press **Stop**, which turns all six off. A program that finishes or errors leaves its outputs as they were.

## Waiting for a signal

There's no redstone event yet, so check once a tick:

```lua
-- wait for a pulse on the front, then do something
while not rs.getInput("front") do
  sleep(0)
end
print("got a pulse")
```

`sleep(0)` waits one tick, so a loop like this costs almost nothing while it waits.
